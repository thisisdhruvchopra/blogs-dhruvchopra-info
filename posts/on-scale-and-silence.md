I was probably nine or ten the first time I really sat with the numbers. Not just heard them, actually sat with them, tried to make them feel real. The distance to Proxima Centauri: 4.24 light-years. The Milky Way: a hundred thousand light-years across. The observable universe: 93 billion light-years in diameter, containing roughly two trillion galaxies, each with hundreds of billions of stars.

I remember the specific sensation that followed: a kind of pleasant vertigo, like the ground was further away than I'd thought. I didn't have a word for it at the time. I've been chasing it ever since.

## The problem with comprehension

The honest answer is that these numbers don't mean anything to human cognition. Not really. We're built to navigate at the scale of roughly one metre to maybe a hundred kilometres. Everything beyond that is mathematical abstraction wearing the costume of a number.

When astrophysicists say the nearest star is 4.24 light-years away, they mean that light, which circles the entire Earth seven times in a single second, would still need more than four years of continuous travel to get there. And Proxima Centauri is the *nearest* star. Our immediate neighbour.

The galaxy is mostly nothing. The universe is mostly nothing. What we call matter is a vanishingly small rounding error in an otherwise empty calculation.

> The universe is not indifferent to us. Indifference implies awareness. The universe is simply vast, and we are simply here, and neither of those facts has anything to say about the other.

## What the scale actually does

I've met people who find this depressing. The smallness of Earth, the brevity of human history against cosmic time, the certainty that the sun will expand and swallow us in approximately five billion years: they find this deflating in a way that makes the present feel meaningless.

I've never understood that reaction. What I feel when I seriously engage with cosmic scale is almost the opposite: a kind of relief. The enormous weight of individual significance deflates in the face of actual infinity. You're not at the centre of anything. Nobody is. And that turns out to be, if you let it, quietly liberating.

## Light as a time machine

When you look at the night sky, you are not seeing the present. You're seeing a palimpsest of different pasts. The Moon as it was about 1.3 seconds ago. The Sun as it was eight minutes ago. The Andromeda Galaxy as it was 2.5 million years ago, before our species existed. Some of the galaxies the James Webb Space Telescope has photographed show us light that left its source over 13 billion years ago.

Looking up is literally looking backward in time. The sky is a fossil record of light. I don't think I'll ever fully get over that.

## On being moved by things you can't change

My day-to-day work is profoundly terrestrial: attack paths, deception systems, log data, threat actors. The opposite of cosmic in scale. And yet I find that keeping one eye on the very large makes the very small more manageable, not less. Scale gives proportion. Proportion gives perspective.

Astrophysics, for me, has always been less a technical interest and more a practice. Something I return to when I need to be reminded of the actual size of things. Which turns out to be fairly often.

The cosmos doesn't care. But somehow, knowing that helps.
